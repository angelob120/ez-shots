Photographing vacant homes: making empty rooms sell

An empty living room with bare windows and no furniture reads tiny. The walls feel closer than they are. Every scratch on the hardwood shows. This is the challenge with vacant listings, and it is why many agents assume furnished homes always photograph better.

Why vacant rooms photograph smaller than they are

Furnished rooms have objects at different distances. A couch in the foreground, a coffee table mid-room, a lamp in the corner. These reference points help your brain judge size. Take all of that away and the room collapses visually. The eye has nothing to anchor on, so it guesses wrong.

This is not just about aesthetics. A listing that looks small online gets fewer showings. Fewer showings mean fewer offers. The photography literally costs you money when a vacant home is not shot well.

  • Use a wider angle than you would in a furnished room. It gives the space breathing room and fights the cramped feel.
  • Shoot from corners rather than the middle of the wall. Corner angles open up the room and show more depth.
  • Include the full door frame and at least a few inches of ceiling. Cropping too tight makes a room feel boxed in.

What makes a vacant room look right

The biggest mistake with vacant listings is shooting too fast. You cannot rely on staging to hide problems, so you have to find angles that work. A bare room forgives nothing. If the light is wrong, everyone sees it. If the white balance is off, the empty walls scream it.

Clean the floors. I mean really clean. In a furnished room, you barely notice scuff marks. In a vacant listing, every mark on the hardwood or scratch on the tile is the hero of the photo. Sweep, mop, and check corners. Then check them again.

  • Turn on every light in the house. Even during the day, interior lights add warmth and eliminate dark corners that look uninviting in photos.
  • Open all blinds and curtains. Natural light is your best friend in vacant listings, and bare windows let in more of it.
  • Check every surface for dust, smudges, or fingerprints. Walls that look fine to the naked eye will flash white under professional lighting.

The furnished advantage is not magic

Furnished homes are easier to shoot, but they also have hidden problems. Furniture hides floor damage, wall marks, and dated fixtures. A vacant listing forces you to address these issues or work around them. Some agents actually prefer photographing vacant homes because there is nothing to move, nothing to arrange, and no risk of the seller's clutter ruining the shots.

The bottom line is that vacant listings need more thought, not less skill. A good photographer adjusts for empty rooms the way they adjust for odd lighting or awkward layouts. It is part of the job.
